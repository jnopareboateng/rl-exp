## Abstract

A fingerprint-similarity score is easy to misread: an answer that ignores the input can earn most of it. We test this in a pre-registered, controls-first study of one task with a known answer, writing an approved drug's structure (SMILES) from a pharmacology profile with the name withheld, on an in-house dataset (351 drugs split by structure component; a temporal cohort of 41 drugs first approved in 2025 and 2026 and a random split of 30). Beside the scores we report a tuned constant answer, a random-pair calibration, a shuffled-profile counterfactual and a rank readout that one fixed answer cannot pass. No system recovers a structure (exact match 0), and no arm of the frozen specification exceeds the best constant answer that could have been chosen, on validation or on either test split (a Val-only profile-free control edges 0.0003 above the validation ceiling): the reinforcement-learning (RL) checkpoint of Qwen3.5-4B scores 0.195 against 0.195, 0.186 against 0.199 on the temporal cohort and 0.168 against 0.171 on the random split. The one registered contrast, that checkpoint against the reward-optimal constant on the temporal cohort, does not reject (difference -0.013, 95% interval -0.026 to +0.0004, 10 wins and 31 losses over 41 drugs, p = 0.061), and its sign reverses on the random split (+0.009, exploratory). RL converged on a small set of generic structures, which fits a reward-spread check that every run failed, and RL trained without the profile reaches the same training reward and, on validation drugs, 0.178 and 0.196 against 0.183 with it. A small profile-dependent signal appears on random held-out drugs and in larger untrained models, is not detected on the temporal cohort, and does not survive multiplicity adjustment. We release the code, splits, control answers and an eleven-item checklist (the in-house profile texts are not released): report a tuned constant, a calibrated scale, the cost, a shuffled-input run, a constant-proof readout and a reward-spread check beside any similarity-rewarded result.


## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and motivation

Language models are increasingly asked to propose molecules from text, and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) is a popular way to improve them: a graded similarity between a generated structure and a reference supplies a cheap, automatic reward. We ask whether that recipe adds anything on a concrete task whose answer is known. The input is a written pharmacology profile of an approved drug with its name withheld; the output is the drug's structure as a SMILES string, scored against the registry structure.

### 1.2 The problem: what a similarity score proves

The difficulty is not training; it is knowing what a score means. A similarity of 0.19 between Morgan count fingerprints sounds like partial success, but a generic drug-like molecule scores about that against any approved drug, so a reward that pays for similarity can be raised by answers that ignore the input. A study of this task therefore has to answer, before it reports any gain, what a zero-cost answer achieves, what a random pair of approved drugs scores, whether the model's answer depends on the drug at all, and what the score costs in samples and tokens.

Neighbouring fields have met the same problem: hypothesis-only baselines that ignore the premise score well above chance on language-inference benchmarks, and ligand-based benchmarks have been shown to reward memorisation (Section 2.3). For similarity-rewarded text-to-structure tasks we found no study that reports the answer of an input-ignoring policy beside the score of a trained one (Section 2.6; this describes our searches, not the literature).

### 1.3 Research questions

We pose five research questions. RQ2 is the study's single confirmatory test, registered before any held-out sample existed; the others are secondary or exploratory (Table 3).

- **RQ1 (ceiling and scale).** How much similarity does an answer that ignores the input earn, on a scale calibrated by random pairs of approved drugs, and does any system exceed it?
- **RQ2 (registered contrast).** Does the validation-selected RL checkpoint deliver higher first-valid similarity, at a stated cost, than the best tuned alternative, which the protocol fixed as the reward-optimal constant answer, on a temporal cohort of drugs first approved in 2025 and 2026?
- **RQ3 (mechanism).** What does RL learn on this task: does the policy converge on a generic structure, and does the reward gain need the input at all?
- **RQ4 (profile use).** Does any system's output depend on the profile beyond what an answer that ignores it earns, and does any system use the profile better than copying the structure of the nearest training profile?
- **RQ5 (cohort dependence).** Do the answers hold on a random held-out split as on the temporal cohort, and do they depend on how long a structure had been public before approval?

### 1.4 Approach in brief

We built the study controls-first and pre-registered it. One endpoint, the similarity of the first valid answer among eight samples at a stated cost (called delivered similarity below), and one confirmatory contrast, the validation-selected (Val) RL checkpoint against the best of six tuned alternatives, are fixed by a git tag before any held-out sample exists. The alternatives include a reward-optimal constant answer chosen from the training molecules and a public drug pool, a copy of the nearest training profile's structure, untrained, retrieval-augmented and supervised fine-tuned models, and the held-out data include a temporal cohort of 41 drugs first approved in 2025 and 2026. Counterfactual and constant-proof readouts, a shuffled-profile run and a rank of the true drug among the split's own references, say whether a model uses the profile when the headline metric cannot.

### 1.5 Summary of findings

We find five things. (1) No system recovers a structure, and no arm of the frozen specification exceeds the best constant answer that could have been chosen, on validation or on either test split; the one exception, a Val-only profile-free control, sits 0.0003 above the validation ceiling (RQ1). (2) The registered contrast does not reject: the validation-selected RL checkpoint delivers 0.186 against 0.199 for the reward-optimal constant on the temporal cohort (p = 0.061), and the sign reverses on the random held-out split (RQ2). (3) RL reached that level by converging on a small set of generic structures, which fits a reward-spread check that every run failed, and RL trained without the profile reaches the same reward and the same validation similarity (RQ3). (4) The profile carries a small drug-specific signal, visible on random held-out drugs and more clearly in the untrained 9B and 27B models, that we could not detect on the temporal cohort and that does not survive a multiplicity adjustment on either test split (RQ4). (5) Approval date is not disclosure date: 40 of the 41 structures of the temporal cohort had a PubChem record a median of 10.5 years before approval (RQ5).

### 1.6 Contributions

Our contributions, and how each relates to prior work (Section 2), are:

1. *A protocol and controls for similarity-scored text-to-structure results*, packaged as an eleven-item checklist (Appendix E): a tuned constant answer, a random-pair calibration, a shuffled-input counterfactual and a rank readout that one fixed answer cannot pass. The constant bar adapts best-from-dataset baselines of molecular-generation benchmarks to a conditional task, and the counterfactual adapts hypothesis-only baselines from language inference; the rank readout, with its exact chance value, we did not find published.
2. *A measurement of what online RL does with a graded similarity reward*: it approaches, and does not exceed, the ceiling of a constant answer, while a validity-only reward degrades to trivially valid answers. A training control that deletes the input and keeps the reward indicates, in two Val-only runs, that the reward gain does not need the input; Spurious Rewards (Shao et al., 2025) randomised the reward, and we did not find the input-deletion control published.
3. *A reward-spread check to run before training*, whose bar was set before any RL here and which every run failed, a result that fits the outcome we observed (Section 4.4).
4. *A temporal cohort with exposure covariates*, and the finding that approval date is not disclosure date.
5. *A pre-registered, hash-attested evaluation* with a deviations log that includes two failed pre-set checks, a cost accounting of the whole study (8.7 GPU-hours), and a release of the code, splits and control answers.

## 2. Related work

### 2.1 Chemistry language models and verifiable rewards

The nearest large-scale work is ether0 (Narayanan et al., 2025), a 24-billion-parameter model trained by supervised fine-tuning and group-relative policy optimisation (GRPO, Shao et al., 2024) on 640,730 verifiable chemistry problems, with mostly binary rewards; its tasks include description-to-SMILES, so mapping text to structure is part of its training, whereas ours is one task with held-out approved drugs, the name withheld, a graded similarity reward and about 240 training molecules. Text-to-molecule benchmarks and models include MolT5 and ChEBI-20 (Edwards et al., 2022), LlaSMol (Yu et al., 2024), ChemLLMBench (Guo et al., 2023) and TOMG-Bench (Li et al., 2026b). Mol-R1 (Li et al., 2025) and MolReasoner (Zhao et al., 2025a) apply GRPO to caption-to-SMILES on ChEBI-20, MolReasoner with a graded fingerprint-similarity reward; Oniani et al. (2024) generate SMILES from drug indications with MolT5 and report exact matches of at most 0.1% and Morgan similarity of at most 0.09, with no controls. Smaller efforts apply RL with RDKit-based rewards to other molecular tasks (LLMol, Ouyang et al., 2026; RePO, Li et al., 2026c; ChemDFM-R, Zhao et al., 2025b; ChemCRAFT, Li et al., 2026a).

### 2.2 What reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards changes

Yue et al. (2025) found that reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards improves pass@1 while base models match or surpass RL models at large k, so RL narrows sampling to solutions the base model could already reach. The claim is contested. ProRL (Liu et al., 2025a) reports that prolonged RL expands what a model can solve, and Zhou (2026) diagnoses a pass@k inversion, in which RL leaves a model able to solve fewer distinct problems than its base model under repeated sampling, and proposes a per-problem anchor to the base model. A second line asks whether the gains come from the reward at all: Spurious Rewards (Shao et al., 2025) found that random or incorrect rewards raised Qwen2.5-Math-7B by 21.4 points on MATH-500 against 29.1 points for ground-truth rewards, an effect that often failed to appear in Llama3 and OLMo2 models, and Wu et al. (2025) argue that the reported RL gains of Qwen2.5 on math benchmarks may be inflated by contamination of pretraining data and find, on a clean arithmetic benchmark, that only accurate rewards improve the model. A study of protein language models (Cao et al., 2025) reports reduced diversity, a base-model advantage at large k on one of its tasks, and gains set by task headroom, reward fidelity and policy capacity. These works share our concern that an RL gain need not come from the signal one intends. They randomise or corrupt the reward; we keep the reward and delete the input (Section 4.4). Our RL runs of 40 to 63 steps are far from prolonged training, so we make no claim about what longer RL would do. Hence we report majority vote and pass@8 apart from the deployable endpoint, and include cheaper methods that also sharpen, such as rejection-sampling fine-tuning, which Xiong et al. (2025) advocate as a robust, interpretable baseline. The recipe behind our RL loop is in Appendix C. <!-- docs/paper/references_audit.md rows 14 to 18; arXiv 2505.24864, 2607.20543, 2506.10947 and 2507.10532 read on 2026-10-18 -->

### 2.3 Baselines, shortcuts and benchmark hygiene

Strong trivial baselines and input ablations are established practice in neighbouring fields. In natural-language inference, a hypothesis-only model that ignores the premise often beats the majority-class baseline across ten datasets (Poliak et al., 2018), and a simple classifier labels the hypothesis alone correctly in about 67% of SNLI and 53% of MultiNLI examples (Gururangan et al., 2018). In molecular generation, GuacaMol (Brown et al., 2019) ships a dummy baseline that returns molecules from the benchmark's training set with no model; Renz et al. (2019) reported that goal-directed generators exploit the models that score them, and Langevin et al. (2022) traced those failures to disagreement between the predictive models used to optimise and to evaluate. In ligand-based virtual screening, Wallach and Heifets (2018) argued that most benchmarks reward memorisation rather than generalisation, and Huang et al. (2025) found that a trivial memorisation baseline can match the state of the art on LIT-PCBA because of leakage between its splits. Our scoring function is exact, a known structure, so the failure we study is not model disagreement but the reward's own generic optimum: a single answer that ignores the input. We adapt the best-from-dataset baseline to a conditional task, one answer for every input chosen to maximise the reward, and the hypothesis-only idea to a shuffled and a deleted input, and we use whole-component splits, as the audits of Wallach and Heifets and of Huang et al. motivate. <!-- arXiv 1706.06619, 2507.21404, 1805.01042 and 1803.02324; PMC8973583; Crossref record of Renz et al.; github.com/BenevolentAI/guacamol_baselines (best_from_chembl), all read on 2026-10-18 -->

### 2.4 Temporal splits and contamination

Time-split validation estimates prospective performance in drug discovery better than random splits (Sheridan, 2013), and recent benchmarks for language models use post-cutoff data for the same reason. AssayBench (De Brouwer et al., 2026) splits CRISPR screens by publication year, with screens published before 2021 for training, those of 2021 for validation and later ones for testing, and adds 19 screens published after September 2025; Test of Time (Zhang et al., 2025) shows that the post-cutoff performance drop often read as contamination evidence depends on how the questions are built. Our temporal cohort, drugs first approved in 2025 and 2026, follows this practice. What we add is a measurement of how long each structure had been public before approval, which shows that a recent approval is not an unseen molecule (Section 4.6). <!-- arXiv 2605.10876 (Section 2.3 of its text) and 2509.00072 read on 2026-10-18 -->

### 2.5 Pre-registration and negative results in machine learning

Pre-registration is rare in machine learning; two NeurIPS workshops, in 2020 and 2021, made the case for it (Bertinetto et al., 2021; Albanie et al., 2022). We apply the practice to an RL evaluation. The specification, the checkpoint selection and the data manifest are fixed by sha256 hashes in an annotated git tag before any held-out sample exists, a driver refuses a specification that differs from the frozen one, an addendum adds analyses without changing arms, and a deviations table records everything that differs from the plan, including two pre-set checks that failed (Section 3.10, Appendix D). Because the tags were created on the authors' machine and pushed to a remote only after the Test run, the order of events is attested by hashes, not by an independent timestamp (Section 6).

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Study design

The study is staged so that every choice that could favour an arm is made before the held-out data are touched (Table 2). Four principles shaped it. *Controls first*: the cheapest answers (constants and a copy of the nearest training profile) and the zero-training models are scored before anything is trained, and RL is a bounded case study entered only after its cheaper alternatives are in place. *One endpoint and one confirmatory contrast*, fixed in advance, with every other comparison labelled secondary or exploratory. *Separation of selection from evaluation*: Train trains, a 20-drug validation split (Val) selects decoding, checkpoints and the comparator, and two held-out splits (Test-T and Test-R) are scored once under a frozen specification. *Counterfactual and constant-proof readouts*, because the endpoint alone cannot tell a system that reads the profile from one that learned a good generic answer. <!-- docs/plan.md sections 2 and 7; docs/prereg-v1.md -->

### 3.3 The task

**Question and task.** Does online reinforcement learning (RL) improve the recovery of held-out drug structures beyond cheaper alternatives, at a stated inference cost? The input is a written pharmacology profile of an approved drug, name withheld. The output is its structure as a SMILES string (a one-line text code for a molecule), scored against the known structure. None of the 991 small-molecule prompts (980 once the 11 two-drug rows are set aside) contains the drug's name, and most state no computed property such as molecular weight or logP (75 to 93% mention none of the properties surveyed). <!-- docs/plan.md section 1 --> Success therefore mixes recall of the drug with reasoning from its profile.

### 3.5 Splits and cohorts

We call the four splits Train, Val (validation), Test-R (random held-out components) and Test-T (the temporal cohort). Table 5 gives their sizes.

**Splits.** Splits are by structure. Drugs join one component when their Tanimoto similarity (the share of substructure features two molecules have in common, from 0 to 1, here on 2,048-bit Morgan fingerprints) reaches 0.4 or they share an identical non-empty Murcko scaffold (the ring systems and the linkers between them); whole components go to one split. Test-T is assigned first and its components' other members (19 drugs) form an unused buffer; whole components then fill Test-R and Val by a seeded shuffle, and the rest is Train. The 351 usable drugs form 302 components, and no pair across splits reaches 0.4 or shares a scaffold (highest cross-split similarity 0.398). <!-- data/processed/split_report.json --> Test-T's drugs sit in 41 distinct components, so each is its own unit in the test of Section 3.9.

### 3.6 Scoring

**Reading the answer.** A model reasons in a thinking block, then answers. The scorer reads only the text after the last closing tag, so an answer counts only if the block is closed (non-thinking runs supply an empty, closed block). From the end of that text it takes the last whitespace-separated piece that, with formatting characters stripped, is 8 to 1,500 characters long, contains a digit or a bond, branch, bracket or chirality character, and parses as a molecule in the RDKit chemistry toolkit. Answer and reference are reduced to their largest fragment and neutralised alike; over 400 atoms or any dummy atom makes an answer invalid. <!-- docs/contracts.md (Chemistry definitions) -->

**Identity.** Two structures are the same drug when they share the first block of the standard InChIKey (formula, connectivity and hydrogen placement, not stereochemistry). The key is strict because a looser one equated real isomers (delta-8 and delta-9 tetrahydrocannabinol) in review; five references carry a curated alternate spelling for registry differences in hydrogen placement. <!-- docs/memory/decision-connectivity-key.md --> Stereochemistry is compared by the full key and reported, never rewarded; the dataset's own answers match registry stereochemistry in only about 89% of rows. <!-- docs/plan.md section 1 -->

**Reward.** The reward is 0 for an answer that is missing, unparseable, from an unclosed thinking block or cut by the length limit, and 1.0 for an identity match. Otherwise it is 0.05 for a valid answer, plus 0.6 times the Tanimoto similarity to the reference, plus 0.3 if the Murcko scaffold equals the reference's non-empty scaffold. <!-- docs/contracts.md (reward.py v2) --> Similarity uses Morgan count fingerprints (radius 2, 2,048 positions), which penalise repeated units that a bit version ignores. <!-- docs/contracts.md --> The weights are provisional, tuned only on Train rollouts and Val. An earlier similarity floor of 0.2 erased the signal, since 98.5% of wrong drug pairs fall at or below 0.2 and group-relative RL learns from reward order within a group; a scaffold bonus replaced it (random drug pairs share a scaffold only 0.033% of the time, 20 of 61,425). <!-- docs/plan.md sections 0 and 1; docs/memory/decision-reward-v2.md -->

**Checks.** The answer reader was audited against a stricter rule ("the last line that is only a SMILES") on 10,304 Val samples from 29 runs: the two disagree on 2.1% of samples, only 0.05% as a different molecule. We kept the reader, because the RL arms were trained against it, and rescore the Test samples with the stricter rule as a sensitivity analysis (Section 4.3); a sample that hits the token limit fails for every arm. Details are in Appendix B.

**Reward-spread check (T5).** A group-relative method, such as REINFORCE with a group baseline or GRPO, learns only from differences in reward between samples for the same prompt. The protocol therefore set a check on the reward itself, before any RL: among the valid, closed, finished answers in a group of eight, at least 30% of prompts should show a reward standard deviation of at least 0.05, so that the reward ranks graded similarity and not only validity. The bar was set before any RL. We measured it on Val samples, at the freeze, for every run with at least four samples per prompt (including the SFT start and RL checkpoints), because Train rollouts of the start policy were not made. The check failed for every run; Section 4.4 gives the numbers and what they point to. <!-- docs/plan.md section 4; results/t5.json; docs/prereg-v1.md section 8 -->

**CPU controls.** These are fixed by Train alone. C0 is the Train structure with the highest mean 2,048-bit Tanimoto similarity to the other Train structures, the do-nothing bar. C1 copies the structure of the Train drug whose profile text is most similar by word overlap (TF-IDF cosine); it is an answer copy, not retrieval-augmented prompting. C2 is the shortest Train structure among those with the most common scaffold. C3, the reward-optimal constant, is the single structure, taken from the Train references or from a public pool of approved drugs (every structure in or near a held-out split removed), with the highest mean Tanimoto similarity to the Train references (a Train candidate is scored without its own entry): what a policy that ignores the profile can at best learn. It maximises the similarity term of the reward; the reward also pays 0.05 for validity, which every constant earns, and 0.3 for a scaffold match, which random drug pairs satisfy 0.033% of the time, so the similarity-optimal constant is reward-optimal up to a negligible term. C0 to C3 are deterministic, so their first-valid similarity equals their mean similarity (each answer is repeated eight times to meet the endpoint's definition). C0 to C2 were scored on every split before the lock, as the protocol states; C3 was defined and scored on Val before the lock (deviation D1), and its Test scores and the eight-sample copies of all four controls were made after it from Train alone; no arm, decoding setting or comparator was chosen with a Test value, and the nearest-Train oracle ceilings (0.28 on Test-T and 0.30 on Test-R) come from reference structures only. <!-- docs/contracts.md (controls.py); scripts/best_constant.py; scripts/replicate_samples.py -->

**Reference ceilings (no model).** Two quantities bound what a method that only picks a Train structure or a pool structure could deliver. The oracle constant of a split is the best single answer that any candidate could have been (the Train references and the pool, 1,828 candidates in all), found by scoring every candidate against that split's own reference structures. The nearest-Train oracle copies, for each drug, the Train structure most similar to its reference. Both use reference structures only: no model output, no prompt and no Test sample enters either. <!-- scripts/constant_ceiling.py; results/constant_ceiling.json; results/constant_ceiling_test.json; docs/plan.md section 5 -->

**Class-information ceiling.** From ChEMBL release 37 we took 1,786 approved drugs in 771 components with coarsened class facts (ATC class, mechanism, target, indication), and predicted each drug's structure from the structures of its neighbours by two rules, the single nearest neighbour and the medoid of the nearest five, drawing neighbours only from other components as the real split requires.

**Model arms at 4B.** Retrieval (A4) puts the three most similar Train profiles, never from the query's own component, in the prompt with their structures. Answer-only SFT (A2) trains rank-32 LoRA adapters on every decoder linear layer to write an empty thinking block and then the reference SMILES; epoch 1 had the lowest Val loss and is the arm. Trace SFT (A1) is Val-only (deviation D2) and rejection-sampling fine-tuning (A3) was not run. Details: Appendix C.

**Diagnostics, not arms.** Name-given replaces the profile by the drug's name, measuring recall for a model that knows which drug it is. Identification keeps the profile and asks for the name, scored by normalised name match; together they split the gap into identification (profile to name) and recall (name to structure). Shuffled-profile shows each row the profile of a donor row of another drug (same task type, same prompt-length tercile, another structural component whenever the stratum allows, found by an exact assignment), and scores against its own reference: an arm that uses the profile should lose first-valid similarity and lift there, and one that emits a good constant should not. The control keeps task type and length, not the section order or wording that differ between prompts of one task type. Blank-profile replaces the profile by "Drug: unknown." for every row, so the prompt is one fixed string and the eight draws are shared by all rows; it describes what the model says with no information about the drug and is read through mean similarity and validity, not through first-valid similarity. <!-- src/rlexp/arms.py; src/rlexp/ident.py; src/rlexp/shuffle.py; docs/memory/gotcha-blank-profile-shared-draws.md -->

**Online RL (A5).** We wrote a small training loop (REINFORCE with a group baseline, prompt-level aggregation, batch-normalised advantages, a 32-bit output head, no KL penalty) that starts from the A2 adapter and draws 24 distinct Train drugs and eight samples each per step (learning rate 2e-5, answers up to 128 tokens). The pilot R1 ran 40 steps, the replicate R5 (another seed) stopped itself at step 63 on a collapse rule, and the checkpoint entering the confirmatory contrast is the saved step with the highest Val delivered similarity when re-sampled with vLLM at eight samples and the arm's chosen temperature. Details: Appendix C.

**Reward modes and controls.** Standard is the reward of Section 3.6. Contrastive subtracts from a valid answer's reward 0.6 times its mean similarity to the other drugs' references in the same step, floored at zero, so a generic answer earns little and no answer scores below a failure. Validity-only gives 1 to a valid, closed, finished answer and 0 otherwise, isolating syntax. Profile-free RL replaces every profile by one fixed string under the same reward, and cold-start RL (not run) would use a fresh adapter instead of the SFT start. <!-- gpu/RL.md; src/rlexp/grpo.py -->

### 3.8 Metrics

**Unit.** The unit is the drug, not the row: a drug's value is the mean over its rows of the mean over each row's samples, and drugs count equally. <!-- docs/contracts.md (metrics.py) -->

**Confirmatory endpoint: first-valid similarity (called delivered similarity in the results).** For each prompt row, draw up to eight answers and keep the first valid one (thinking block closed, molecule parses, sample not cut by the length limit). The row scores that answer's Tanimoto similarity to the true structure, or 0 if none qualifies; scores are averaged within drugs, then across drugs. This is the deployable policy "resample until the chemistry toolkit accepts the SMILES". It compares arms at matched validity without conditioning on it: expected similarity over all attempts mostly measures whether a model writes parseable SMILES, and a mean over valid answers only rewards failing on hard cases, which review rejected. Its cost is reported beside it: mean generations until the first valid answer (8 if none) and generated tokens per answer, retrieval and retries included (prompt tokens are not counted, which flatters the retrieval arm, whose prompt is longer). <!-- docs/contracts.md (metrics.py); docs/memory/decision-endpoint-fv-tanimoto.md --> The plan first named core recovery (an identity match or a shared non-empty scaffold). Val showed exact recovery at 0 and core recovery at most 5% for every arm, so a test on either would be decided by ties; after a second independent review we replaced it, using Val data only. <!-- docs/plan.md section 2; docs/memory/gotcha-core-endpoint-low-power.md; results/val_baselines.json -->

**Secondary metrics** (never primary; per sample, averaged as above). *Expected similarity*: Tanimoto over all attempts, invalid counting 0. *Validity*: share of samples valid, closed and finished. *Valid-only similarity*: mean over valid samples. *Exact recovery*: identity match. *Core recovery*: identity match or a shared non-empty scaffold. *Stereo-exact*: full-key match. *Majority vote* (maj@8): the most common structure among the first eight answers is the true one, ties earning nothing; deployable. *Pass@8*: at least one sample is a hit; picking it needs the answer, so it is an oracle upper bound. <!-- docs/contracts.md (metrics table) -->

**Profile-specific lift.** For a valid answer to drug d on a split, lift is its similarity to d's own reference minus its mean similarity to the other drugs' references; any other sample has lift 0. A constant answer valid on every row has lift exactly 0 over a complete panel. The zero holds only if completion (valid, closed, finished) does not depend on the drug; otherwise a fixed molecule can score above zero (+0.18 in a synthetic case where only one drug finishes). Lift measures association between the drug and everything the policy does, nuisance cues included, and depends on the drug panel: a diagnostic. <!-- docs/contracts.md (Lift, v3) -->

**Rank readout (registered in the addendum before any Test sample of a model arm was scored).** The endpoint is maximised by an answer that ignores the drug, so it cannot tell a system that reads the profile from one that learned a good generic molecule. For every prompt row we take the endpoint's own answer (the first valid, closed, finished one among eight samples), rank the split's N reference structures by Tanimoto similarity to it, and record where the row's own drug lands (ties share their mid-rank; a row with no valid answer takes the worst rank, and the share of rows with an answer is reported beside the result). Per drug we average over rows, then over drugs, and report the top-1 rate and the mean percentile rank (lower is better). One fixed answer that is valid on every row scores exactly 1/N on top-1 (at most that when its nearest references tie) and exactly (N+1)/2N on percentile rank, whatever molecule it is; failures and ties only lower it, and a drug-independent policy whose answers vary scores those values in expectation (sd 0.02 to 0.035 on Val), so the number of distinct answers is reported too. Contrasts use the same paired cluster sign-flip test as the endpoint. <!-- docs/prereg-v1-addendum1.md section 2; docs/contracts.md (rank_readout.py); scripts/rank_readout.py -->

**Random-pair calibration.** To place a similarity on the scale of the metric we computed the Tanimoto similarity of every pair of the 332 distinct reference structures of the four used splits (54,946 pairs; reference structures only): mean 0.141, median 0.141, 75th percentile 0.180, 90th 0.213, 99th 0.274. This is a scale over the study's drugs after a split rule that removes cross-split near neighbours, not a null distribution for a delivered similarity. <!-- results/random_pair_calibration.json; scripts/random_pair_calibration.py -->

### 3.9 Statistical analysis

**The confirmatory contrast (H1).** The Val-selected RL checkpoint (standard or contrastive reward) against the best comparator by Val first-valid similarity (the constant, copy-nearest, reward-optimal constant, untrained, retrieval or answer-only SFT arm, each at its Val-selected decoding), on Test-T. The test is an exact two-sided sign-flip permutation test on per-drug differences at level 0.05, with wins, losses and ties shown. Related drugs repeat one outcome, so differences are summed within each component and the component totals are sign-flipped (all patterns up to 20 non-tied units, else 100,000 random flips). <!-- docs/prereg-v1.md section 4 -->

Its null is that each component's summed difference is symmetric about zero, which is stronger than a zero mean, with independent components; it is not assumption-free. A studentized line (mean difference over its bootstrap standard error) and 95% percentile bootstrap intervals resampling whole components (10,000 resamples; 2,000 for Val tables) are descriptive only. <!-- docs/contracts.md; scripts/val_table.py --> All else is secondary (the addendum's S1 to S4) or exploratory, without multiplicity adjustment, including H2 (a gain persists on Test-R, not only in the best-exposed third of Test-T) and H3 (baselines improve with size, identification most). Training seeds are few (Section 6). <!-- docs/paper/README.md --> The Test evaluation runs through a driver that refuses a specification whose hash differs from the one recorded at the freeze, a setting outside the frozen grid, and any split other than the two Test splits. <!-- gpu/final_eval.py; docs/prereg-v1.md section 6 -->

**Power and claims.** We made no formal power calculation. With n non-tied units the smallest two-sided p-value is 2 to the power 1 minus n: five non-tied drugs can never go below 0.0625 (five wins among 41 drugs with 36 ties give exactly that), and six that agree give 0.031. <!-- docs/contracts.md (the five-wins example); the general form is arithmetic from the test definition --> A metric on which most drugs tie, as exact and core recovery do, cannot be tested. For first-valid similarity, the standard deviation of the paired per-drug differences between arms on Val is 0.04 to 0.08 (20 drugs), so with 41 drugs a paired test at the 5% level and 80% power detects a difference of roughly 0.02 to 0.04 between arms (2.8 standard deviations over the square root of 41; the within-arm shuffle contrast of Section 4.5 has a smaller paired standard deviation and so a smaller minimum, 0.012 to 0.015); the Val gap between the selected RL checkpoint and the best constant is 0.008 (0.002 for the pilot), far inside that. Non-significance is not evidence of no effect. <!-- docs/plan.md section 2; consistency-pass computation from results/val_baselines.json inputs (paired per-drug SDs 0.040-0.083, MDD 0.017-0.036) -->

The allowed claim is "higher delivered similarity at a stated cost, against specified tuned controls, on this split and cohort". The protocol tied the word "profile" to three conditions at once: lift above zero with a bootstrap interval excluding zero, lift above the copy-nearest control, and a shuffled-profile run with lower first-valid similarity than the correct profile. The second condition asks whether an arm uses the profile better than TF-IDF retrieval, not whether it uses it, and the lift is biased for near-constant policies, so the addendum, registered before any Test sample of a model arm was scored, splits the word into two claims. **P1, "the arm's output depends on the profile"**, needs the matched shuffled-profile run to score lower on the same prompts (paired cluster sign-flip, alpha 0.05, on delivered similarity) and the same direction on the rank readout. **P2, "the arm uses the profile better than nearest-profile copying"**, needs P1 and a lift above the copy-nearest control's with a bootstrap interval for the difference excluding zero. P1 can be claimed only for the two arms with a shuffled-profile run (A2 and RL-full), and neither implies structure recovery. Not claimed: structure recovery (unless exact or core recovery also rise), novel-drug discovery, biological efficacy, general superiority or failure of RL, or molecules unseen in pretraining. <!-- docs/prereg-v1.md sections 1 and 7; docs/prereg-v1-addendum1.md section 3 -->

**Post-hoc sensitivity analyses and equivalence.** After the Test results were known, two kinds of analysis were added that the protocol did not register, and they are labelled post hoc wherever they appear: a plain two-sided sign test on the win and loss counts of a paired contrast (it counts drugs and ignores the size of their differences, unlike the registered test), comparisons between the two held-out cohorts (Section 4.6), and a Holm adjustment over the eight Test cells of the shuffled-profile contrasts (Section 4.5). They are reported beside the registered result and never replace it. No equivalence margin was pre-specified, so a non-rejection is read through the interval and never as equivalence.

### 3.10 Pre-registration, locking and deviations

The protocol was frozen by the annotated git tag `prereg-v1`, whose message carries the sha256 hashes of the frozen specification (`results/frozen_arms.json`), the Val selection (`results/selection.json`) and the data manifest (`data/MANIFEST.sha256`). It fixes the arms, the comparator set, the decoding grid, the checkpoint rule, the endpoint, the confirmatory contrast and the test. The locked driver refuses a specification, a setting outside the frozen grid, a data file or a split other than the two Test splits whose hash or identity differs from the tag's, and before any Test sampling each merged adapter arm must reproduce its Val score within 0.005 (a sanity gate, passed at the first attempt by both adapter arms; Section 4.1). After the tag it is not allowed to look at Test results to change an arm, a decoding setting, the endpoint, a selection rule or the comparator set, to re-score an arm with a different setting, or to add arms; a second sampling run of a frozen arm would be a deviation, and the first complete locked run is the result. Val-only mechanism controls may be run after the tag because they cannot change any of that. The addendum `prereg-v1-a1` added the secondary analyses of Sections 3.8 and 3.9 before any Test sample of a model arm was scored or read, and changed no arm, setting or endpoint. Everything that differs from the plan is listed in Appendix D, including two pre-set checks that failed. The tags were created on the authors' machine and pushed to a remote only after the Test run, so the order of events is attested by the hashes and by the files' own timestamps and not by an independent timestamp. <!-- docs/prereg-v1.md sections 5, 6, 8 and 9; docs/prereg-v1-addendum1.md; gpu/FINAL.md -->

### 4.3 RQ2: the registered contrast, and what RL bought

**Answer.** The registered contrast does not reject. The validation-selected RL checkpoint delivers 0.186 against 0.199 for the reward-optimal constant on the 41 temporal-cohort drugs (p = 0.061), and the interval excludes any material superiority of RL.

**The confirmatory contrast does not reject.** The Val-selected RL checkpoint delivers 0.186 on the 41 temporal-cohort drugs against 0.199 for the reward-optimal constant C3, the registered comparator: difference -0.013 (95% bootstrap interval -0.026 to +0.0004), RL ahead on 10 drugs and behind on 31, cluster sign-flip p = 0.061 (Monte Carlo, 100,000 flips, alpha 0.05, two-sided). The claim "higher delivered similarity than the best comparator" is not supported, and the point estimate points the other way. The reading does not depend on the answer reader: under the strict reader the confirmatory pair is unchanged (RL-full is identical to seven decimals; the strict rescoring moves only A2 slightly: valid share 0.513 to 0.508, expected similarity 0.0709 to 0.0700, the shuffle contrast -0.0019 to -0.0012). An independent recomputation from the scored files (numpy only) reproduces the difference, the 10 wins and 31 losses and the interval, with p = 0.0595 at 400,000 flips. On Test-R, an exploratory comparison, RL is ahead of C3 by 0.009 (0.168 against 0.159; 21 wins, 9 losses, p = 0.28). <!-- results/confirmatory.json (paired.test_t, paired.test_r); results/paired_strict_test_t.json; results/confirmatory_recompute.json -->

**Reading the interval.** The interval summarises the contrast better than the p-value does. The 95% bootstrap interval of the difference, -0.026 to +0.0004, excludes any material superiority of RL over the registered constant on this cohort, and 31 of 41 drugs favour the constant. It does not establish equivalence: its lower limit is larger than the gaps among several arms in Table 8, so a materially worse RL is compatible with the data. The contrast could not reliably detect a difference as small as the one observed (the minimum detectable difference at 41 drugs is about 0.02 to 0.04, Section 3.9), but it is the interval, not the power calculation, that bounds the claim. The result also does not lean on the p-value for its weight: on the temporal cohort the reward-optimal constant is the best of the 1,828 candidate constants (Table 10), so no other candidate would have scored higher there, and the trained policy ended below it. <!-- results/confirmatory.json; results/confirmatory_recompute.json (interval -0.0265 to +0.0005 in the independent recomputation) -->

**Post-hoc sensitivity.** The registered test weights each drug by the size of its difference, so a few large RL wins offset many small losses, and it is the most conservative of three readings of the same data. A plain two-sided sign test on 10 wins and 31 losses gives p = 0.0015 (post hoc), the expected similarity over all samples gives -0.015 at p = 0.029 (secondary, unadjusted), and the registered test gives p = 0.061. We do not use either of the first two to change the registered conclusion. The direction is the same under all three readings and the size of the difference is small; and on Test-R, where RL is ahead of C3 on 21 of 30 drugs, the same sign test gives p = 0.043 in the other direction, so the two held-out splits disagree under every reading. <!-- results/paired_test_t.json and results/paired_test_r.json (win and loss counts, mean_tanimoto); exact binomial tail computed from the counts -->

**What RL bought, and what it cost.** Against the other model arms RL is far ahead on Test-T: +0.054 over its own starting point A2 (37 wins, 4 losses), +0.045 over retrieval A4 (32, 9) and +0.113 over the untrained model (40, 1), each p < 0.001, and it is also the cheapest model arm: one sample and 34 tokens per answer against 2.0 and 92 for A2, 2.4 and 360 for A4 and 5.5 and 761 for the untrained model; 99% of its samples are valid against 51%, 53% and 14%. Against the arms that cost nothing it is not ahead: +0.002 over C0, the Train-only constant (24 wins, 17 losses, p = 0.78), +0.018 over the nearest-profile copy C1 (30, 11; p = 0.028) and -0.013 against C3. The same ordering holds on Test-R (RL ahead of A2, A4 and the untrained model with p between 0.00001 and 0.007, and of the constants by 0.003 to 0.009, p above 0.27). Both arms answer every Test-T row within eight draws, so the gain in delivered similarity lies in the quality of the first valid answer (the similarity of valid answers moves from 0.137 to 0.185) and not in answering more rows; validity (51% of A2's samples against 99% of RL's) buys the cost (2.0 against 1.0 samples per answer) and the gap in expected similarity (0.071 against 0.184). So what RL did on this task was to raise the quality of the first valid answer to the level of a Train-only constant, at the cost of 0.8 to 1.5 GPU-hours per run (Section 3.11). <!-- results/paired_test_t.json; results/paired_test_r.json -->

**Selection optimism.** The checkpoint reached 0.195 on Val, the best of 21 candidates on those 20 drugs. On held-out drugs it delivers 0.186 and 0.168 while the constant C3 delivers 0.199 and 0.159: a Val margin of +0.008 over C3 became -0.013 and +0.009, a margin that changes sign between splits and is not distinguishable from zero in either.

**Selection noise.** The comparator and the arms were not chosen with the same information. C3 is a deterministic function of the 241 Train drugs and a pool of 1,587 approved drugs, whereas every model arm's temperature and checkpoint were chosen on the 20 Val drugs, a noisier selector. A better candidate than the one selected may therefore have existed, and the Val margin of RL over C3 (+0.008) did not carry to Test-T. This favours the constant in expectation; we cannot size it.

The profile-free runs did not follow the protocol of R5 exactly. The collapse stop rule was switched off for them, because a single constant answer is their best policy, so R4 and R4b ran their 40 steps and both concentrated hard, while R5 stopped itself at step 63 when it collapsed. The comparison is therefore profile-free RL allowed to collapse against profile RL stopped when it collapsed. That is the right comparison for asking whether the reward needs the input, but it is not a matched one.

**The reward-spread check (T5).** The protocol set a bar for the reward before any RL: at least 30% of prompts should show a reward standard deviation of at least 0.05 among the valid answers of a group of eight (Section 3.6). The measurement was made on Val samples at the freeze, over runs that include the SFT start and RL checkpoints, and every run failed it: the best was 15.5% of the 58 Val prompts (the SFT model at T=0.6), 5.2% for A2 at T=1.0, the RL sampling temperature, and 0 to 5.2% for the RL checkpoints. Counting invalid answers as reward 0 instead, the spread is large for A2 (91% of prompts at T=1.0) and shrinks as RL proceeds (31%, 17% and 5% of prompts at steps 20, 30 and 40, at T=1.0), so the advantage a group-relative method could use lay between valid and invalid answers and between generic and unusual structures, not between a drug's right and wrong neighbours. The start policy fails the bar, and the outcome we observed (a validity-only reward went to short trivially valid answers, the similarity reward to a generic structure) is the one the check points to. We cannot say that it predicted the outcome, because the measurement in the record was made after the pilot RL run; we report it as a diagnostic that others can run before spending GPU time (item 11 of the checklist, Appendix E), not as a failed gate. <!-- results/t5.json (rows_spread, share and share_all_samples per run); docs/plan.md section 4 -->

### 4.5 RQ4: does any system use the profile?

**Answer.** Showing the profile of another drug lowers delivered similarity by a small amount in several systems (RL +0.012 and A2 +0.024 on Val, RL +0.013 on Test-R, the untrained 9B and 27B +0.040 and +0.049 on Val) and by nothing we can detect on the temporal cohort (RL +0.004, A2 -0.002). No system is shown to carry more drug-specific information than copying the structure of the nearest training profile, and no Test cell survives a multiplicity adjustment.

**Deleting the profile costs a tenth of the score, and a part of that is not drug-specific.** A blank-profile run shows every row the same prompt, so its first-valid value is one shared molecule and only its mean similarity is read. ### 4.6 RQ5: do the answers depend on the cohort, and on exposure?

**Answer.** The contrast with the constant changes sign between the two held-out splits (-0.013 on Test-T, +0.009 on Test-R), the profile effect is detected on the random split and not on the temporal cohort, and the two cohorts differ in several measurable ways at once; the data do not say which matters. H2 (a gain that persists on Test-R and is not concentrated in the best-exposed third) has no gain over the best comparator on Test-T to test, and on Test-R the lead of RL over C3 (+0.009) is not distinguishable from zero (p = 0.28).

**How the two held-out splits differ.** Read-only, exploratory comparisons (medians; permutation tests over rows or drugs) show that the temporal cohort differs from Test-R in several ways at once: its prompts are longer (790 against 594 tokens per row, p = 0.001), its PubChem records are later (median 2014.5 against 2006, over the 40 and 29 drugs with a record, p = 0.003) and it has far fewer PubMed articles (67 against 355, p < 0.001). Its reference structures are more crowded (68% of its drugs have another reference above 0.25 similarity, against 40% on Test-R and 30% on Val), and the headroom of a policy that could pick the best Train structure for each drug over the constant C3 is smaller (median 0.086 against 0.124 on Test-R and 0.120 on Val), although its nearest-Train similarity is the same (0.291 against 0.288, p = 0.77) and its structures are not measurably larger (median 34 heavy atoms against 31, p = 0.13). Recomputing the Test-T readout on random 30-drug candidate subsets, the size of Test-R, leaves it unchanged (RL real minus shuffled -0.010, A2 +0.043), so the size of the candidate set does not explain it. What the data allow is that the two splits differ in several measurable ways and this study cannot say which matters. <!-- results/exploratory_cohort_diagnostics.json (headroom, crowding, subset_readout, composition) -->

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Principal findings

Five results carry the paper, and none depends on a p-value near 0.05. (1) No system recovers a structure: exact match is 0 for every arm on every split. (2) No frozen arm exceeds the best constant answer that could have been chosen, on validation or on either test split (Table 10; the one Val-only exception is stated in Section 4.2); on the temporal cohort the reward-optimal constant is the best of the 1,828 candidate constants (0.199) and the trained policy ended below it (0.186). (3) The policy's answers collapsed onto a few structures: 47 distinct first-valid answers over 100 rows, one structure in 19% of its usable answers, and a similarity of 0.475 between answers to different drugs against 0.135 before RL. (4) The reward gain appears not to need the profile: two RL runs trained with the profile deleted follow the same training reward and end at 0.178 and 0.196 on Val against 0.183 at the same step with the profile (0.195 for the selected checkpoint), and deleting the profile at test time costs the trained policy a tenth of its mean similarity (0.018, interval 0.009 to 0.027 on Test-T), 60% of which showing another drug's profile recovers. (5) A reward-spread check whose bar was set before training, and which every run failed, fits this outcome. The registered contrast, which did not reject (0.186 against 0.199, p = 0.061, interval -0.026 to +0.0004), is a check on these results and not their support: its interval excludes any material superiority of RL and does not establish equivalence. What RL did achieve is real but is a comparison among language models: +0.054 over the supervised model it started from and +0.113 over the untrained model, at 34 tokens per answer against 92 and 761, while on the temporal cohort the same 0.20 is available for free by answering one fixed molecule.

### 5.2 Why RL stopped where it did

The reward pays for similarity, and a drug-independent answer captures almost all of what is available: the best constant any candidate could have been is 0.195 on Val and 0.199 on Test-T. Within a group of eight valid answers the reward ranks little (Section 4.4), so the advantage a group-relative method can use lies between valid and invalid answers and between generic and unusual structures; the policy moved to a generic structure and, once there, stopped improving. A validity-only reward moved the other way, to short, trivially valid answers (0.078 on Val), and the profile-free runs show that the generic structure is reachable with no input. We observed the convergence and ran that control; we did not test the mechanism further (for example with a reward constructed to have no generic optimum), and "learned a generic structure" describes what the policy did, not why.

The finding is specific to this recipe and budget: answer-only RL of 40 to 63 steps, one learning rate, 24 prompts by eight samples per step, and a reward whose within-group spread is the one measured in Section 4.4. Prolonged RL, a reward with no generic optimum (the contrastive run was lost at about step 28 and never repeated) or thinking-mode RL might behave differently, and nothing here tests them.

### 5.3 What the profile contains

A small, drug-specific signal exists. Replacing the profile with another drug's lowers delivered similarity by 0.012 for RL and 0.024 for A2 on Val, by 0.040 and 0.049 for the untrained 9B and 27B on Val, and by 0.013 for RL on random held-out drugs (p = 0.010, which does not survive an adjustment over the eight Test cells); no arm shows it on the temporal cohort (+0.004 and -0.002), although the difference between the two held-out splits is not itself established for delivered similarity (Section 4.5). On Val there is no sign that RL added it: RL runs that never saw a profile retain as much of it from the SFT start (+0.001 and +0.011) as the profile-trained checkpoint shows (+0.012), and all are below A2's +0.024; on Test-T, though, RL's lift exceeds A2's and its readout is better, so whether RL changes the dependence on the profile is not settled. A copy of the structure of the nearest training profile is as good as any trained 4B arm on the rank readout, and retrieval is the one arm whose lift excludes zero on the temporal cohort; the untrained 27B depends on the profile most and ranks the true drug best on Val (0.379), yet delivers only the constant's 0.186. These are small effects on small samples with no multiplicity adjustment, and the temporal cohort differs from the random split in several measurable ways at once, so we do not claim the signal disappears for new drugs, only that this study could not detect it.

The blank-profile result for A2 shows why expected similarity cannot serve as an endpoint. Deleting the profile raises A2's mean similarity from 0.071 to 0.117 on 40 of the 41 temporal-cohort drugs, mostly through validity (0.51 to 0.74), while the similarity of its first valid answer does not change (0.131 against 0.137). A metric that counts invalid answers as zero rewards a prompt that makes the model write parseable SMILES, whatever the chemistry. <!-- results/paired_blank_test_t.json (A2 against A2-blank: mean_tanimoto, fv_tanimoto, valid_closed, tanimoto_valid) -->

### 5.4 Implications for practice

First, compute the reward-optimal constant on the training set (and a public pool) before training anything, and treat it as the number to beat: here it cost nothing and was never beaten. Second, place every score on the metric's own scale: 0.195 is the 83rd percentile of random pairs of approved drugs and 0.187 the 79th, and even an oracle that copies, for each drug, its nearest training structure reaches only 0.31. Third, report delivered similarity with its cost in samples and tokens, because a model can raise the score by paying in draws. Fourth, run a shuffled-input counterfactual and a readout that one fixed answer cannot pass (the rank of the true target among the split's own references), and delete the input at test time and in training once, to see how much of the score survives. Fifth, prefer a ranking, identification or retrieval framing to free generation when the metric is a similarity: on Val the untrained 27B puts the true drug first among 20 about four times as often as chance (top-1 0.196 against 0.050) while delivering no more similarity than a constant, and a nearest-profile copy ranks the true drug as well as any trained 4B arm. Sixth, before spending GPU time on a group-relative method, measure the share of prompts whose valid answers differ in reward (Section 4.4): where it is small, the method can learn validity and genericness and little else. The checklist in `docs/controls_checklist.md` (Appendix E) turns these into eleven items with the number each gave here.

### 5.5 Scope, and what would change the conclusion

This is one task, one in-house dataset of 351 drugs with 241 for training, one 4B model on Test, answer-only RL of at most 63 steps and one training seed per arm beyond the two RL runs; the 9B and 27B models are Val-only. Richer profiles, a larger training set, a larger model, thinking-mode RL or a reward that pays for discrimination instead of similarity could change the result. A contrastive reward was tried and cut short by a runtime loss at about step 28, below its start on Val; a complete rank-based reward is untested. The constant is not a strawman: it is the policy for which the similarity reward is optimal when the input is ignored, which is why RL converged toward it. A public-data version of the task (database text in, structure out) would let others run this protocol without our in-house prompts; we release the splits, control answers and checklist as the first step.


## 6. Limitations and threats to validity

### 6.1 Statistical conclusion validity

**Few training seeds; few drugs.** The SFT arms and the retrieval arm have one training run each. The RL-full arm has two (seeds 0 and 1), which agree at step 40 on Val (0.189 and 0.183 at T=0.6, within the drug-sampling interval of about 0.02) and diverge afterwards only in how far they go before collapsing; the checkpoint that reaches Test is the better of the replicate's saved steps, selected on Val, so its Val score is optimistic. Seed variance of the trained arms is otherwise unmeasured, so differences between them cannot be separated from training noise, and the sign-flip test is over drugs, not seeds. Test-T has 41 drugs and Val 20 (in 16 components): only large, consistent effects can be detected, and choices made on Val are noisy. <!-- docs/prereg-v1.md section 4; results/val_baselines.json -->

**Val-guided choices.** Temperature, SFT checkpoint, RL learning rate and RL checkpoint are all chosen on the same 20 Val drugs, which favours the arms tuned most and can overfit Val; capping each search at three settings limits this but does not remove it. The mechanism controls (the shuffled-profile runs of the 9B and 27B, profile-free RL, validity-only RL) are single Val-only runs on the same 20 drugs.

**The readout, the shuffle and multiplicity.** The rank readout's chance values are exact only for one fixed answer valid on every row; a drug-independent policy whose answers vary scores them in expectation, with a spread of 0.02 to 0.035 on Val, so we report the number of distinct answers and claim no arm beats chance without a test. Failures take the worst rank, so the percentile mixes validity with discrimination and is read with the valid-answer share. The shuffled-profile contrast tests dependence but is not an effect size, because a shuffled answer follows its donor drug, which is itself a candidate; its wins count drugs while its test flips components. S3 compares deployed policies at different temperatures. The four addendum hypotheses (two measures each), the exploratory pairwise contrasts of Section 4.3 and the exposure terciles carry no multiplicity adjustment, and the one registered contrast is the only confirmatory result. <!-- docs/plan.md section 0 (review log); docs/prereg-v1-addendum1.md section 5 -->

**The registered test and the oracle.** The registered sign-flip test is the most conservative reading of the confirmatory data (Section 4.3), no equivalence margin was pre-specified, and the oracle constant is chosen with each split's own drugs in view. The interval reading of Section 4.3 and the ceiling of Section 4.2 are therefore reported beside the registered p-value and not instead of it, and the post-hoc sign test is labelled as such.

### 6.2 Internal validity

**The RL result is specific to this recipe.** The RL runs are answer-only, 40 to 63 steps of 24 prompts by eight samples at one learning rate (the protocol allowed a search over three and none was run), about 4 GPU-hours in all; prolonged RL (Liu et al., 2025a) is the opposite regime. The one reward designed to remove the generic optimum, the contrastive reward, was lost to a runtime failure at about step 28 of 40 and never repeated, so the account of Section 4.4 is descriptive and not causal. <!-- docs/prereg-v1.md section 9 (D3); gpu/RL.md; Appendix C -->

**The profile-free runs are not a matched protocol.** They ran with the collapse stop rule switched off, unlike R5 (Section 4.4), and both seeds are Val-only runs on the same 20 drugs.

**Quantisation and precision.** The 27B models are 8-bit floating-point checkpoints while the 4B and 9B models run in 16-bit (bf16), so the size comparison also changes precision, and no 16-bit 27B run exists to separate the two. Sampled and training log-probabilities differ by 0.015 to 0.018 per token even with a 32-bit output head, and the RL arm is trained with one sampling engine and evaluated with another. <!-- gpu/RL.md; docs/plan.md section 6 -->

**Answer reader and conventions.** The reader misreads 11 of 77 test formats and cannot read very short SMILES, and the length-limit rule discards some usable-looking answers (2.1% of Val samples read differently by a stricter rule, 0.05% as a different molecule; Section 3.6 and Appendix B). Metal and radiolabelled complexes (4 drugs in the used splits) are scored on their organic ligand, and stereochemistry is not rewarded. <!-- data/processed/refs.jsonl -->

### 6.3 Construct validity

**Cohort versus novelty.** Approval date is not disclosure date, the cohort's PubChem records predate approval by years, and the pretraining cutoffs of all four models are undocumented, so we cannot say which structures a model has seen. The regulator audit had medium confidence for 35 of its 43 drugs, so "first approved anywhere" means "no earlier approval found". <!-- data/curated/approval_audit.json; data/curated/approval_audit_notes.md -->

**Class ceiling and lift.** Only two retrieval rules over coarsened facts were tested (its Train subset has 231 drugs), so the result does not bound learned predictors, richer facts or free text. <!-- results/class_ceiling.json --> Lift is constant-invariant only if completion does not depend on the drug, nuisance cues such as prompt length can raise it, and it depends on the panel; any claim about the profile requires the shuffled-profile control.

**Two held-out splits that disagree.** Test-T and Test-R gave different profile effects and differ in prompt length, publication count, record date and crowding (Section 4.6); with 41 and 30 drugs and one task, this study cannot say which of those, if any, matters, and the differences between splits in Table 8 and Table 9 are not themselves tested.

**The endpoint as a construct.** An endpoint that an answer ignoring the input nearly maximises is a weak construct for structure recovery. We kept it because it is the registered, deployable measure (the first valid answer of up to eight tries) and paired it with the constant bar and the rank readout; recovery itself would need a different endpoint (exact or core recovery, a ranking or an identification task), and exact recovery was zero for every arm on every split.

### 6.4 External validity

**One task, one dataset, one model family.** The evidence is one task, one dataset of 351 drugs with 241 for training, and one 4B model family on Test; the larger models are Val-only. The task is hard rather than degenerate: the median gain of picking, for each drug, the best Train structure over the constant C3 is 0.086 on Test-T, 0.124 on Test-R and 0.120 on Val, so recoverable signal exists that no system found. But a negative result in a regime where nothing recovers a structure says little about RL or similarity rewards in general. <!-- results/exploratory_cohort_diagnostics.json (headroom) -->

**Reward.** A similarity reward can be gamed by plausible generic answers, which the constant bar, the contrastive mode and the collapse score are meant to expose. All RL here is answer-only, so nothing is claimed about thinking-mode RL. <!-- docs/paper/README.md; docs/plan.md section 8 -->

**The in-house dataset.** Its provenance is undocumented (who wrote the traces, with which tool and under which terms, what the four prompt formats mean, where 155 protein-related rows came from), its traces were written with the answer known, and it cannot be released, so nobody else can rerun the full pipeline or inspect the profiles. The profiles are characterised only by a keyword survey (most state no computed property) and by the agreement of the dataset's answers with the registry structures. <!-- docs/plan.md section 1; Section 8 (record gaps) -->

### 6.5 Reproducibility and independence

**Independent review.** The plan, metrics, reward and training packs were reviewed by independent reviewers, mostly from another model family (five rounds). That reviewer became unavailable on 2026-10-17, and later rounds used a separate same-vendor reviewer with fresh context, which is less independent. The rank readout and the calibration script were written by an advisor model and reviewed once by a second reviewer, whose findings (a claim that was over-stated and several missing input guards) were addressed after the addendum was tagged and before the readout was run on Test (Appendix D). <!-- docs/memory/decision-review-policy.md; docs/plan.md section 0 -->

All reviews were by AI models; none was a peer review by a human expert. An external review of the finished paper by a further model (Section 8.5) shaped the present structure and wording; it is not a peer review either.

**Timing of the freeze.** The tags `prereg-v1` and `prereg-v1-a1` were created on the authors' machine and pushed to a remote only after the Test run, so the order of events is attested by the hashes in the tag messages and by the files' own timestamps, not by an independent timestamp (Section 3.10).

## 7. Conclusion

We asked what a similarity score proves about a system that writes a drug's structure from its pharmacology profile, and answered it with controls before claims. A constant answer that ignores the profile earns 0.187 on validation, 0.199 on the temporal cohort and 0.159 on the random split, (the 79th and 85th percentile of random pairs of approved drugs on the first two splits), and no frozen system exceeds the best constant that could have been chosen on validation or on either test split (RQ1). The one registered contrast did not reject, its interval excludes any material superiority of RL, and its sign reverses on the random split (RQ2). RL reached the constant's level by converging on generic structures, in line with a reward-spread check that every run failed, and two profile-free runs reached the same reward (RQ3). The profile carries a small drug-specific signal that we detected on random held-out drugs and in larger untrained models and could not detect on the temporal cohort (RQ4), and approval date is not disclosure date (RQ5).

For practice, the lesson is to report a tuned constant, a calibrated scale, the cost, a shuffled-input run, a constant-proof readout and a reward-spread check beside any similarity-rewarded result (Appendix E). For research, the lesson is narrower: this study shows what one recipe did on one task, and it was not designed to show that RL cannot help. The next steps, in order of value per cost, are to run the constant bar, the calibration and the rank readout on a public text-to-molecule benchmark, which needs CPU only and would show whether the bar is as high there; to repeat a contrastive or rank-based reward that has no generic optimum, which was lost here; to add RL seeds and a learning-rate search; and to register a new temporal cohort of drugs approved after this freeze, with an equivalence margin set in advance. Releasing the profile texts would need their provenance and terms to be established first.

## Appendix C. Arm details and the RL recipe

**Model arms at 4B (details).** Answer-only SFT (A2) trains rank-32 LoRA adapters on every decoder linear layer (learning rate 2e-4, batches of 16 rows, at most three epochs) to write an empty thinking block and then the reference SMILES. The lowest-Val-loss checkpoint is epoch 1 (Val loss 1.25, then 1.61 and 1.72, while training loss fell from 0.99 to 0.14): later epochs only memorise the 241 Train drugs. <!-- gpu/SFT.md; docs/plan.md section 5 --> Trace SFT (A1) is the same recipe on the dataset's reasoning traces cut after the answer line. Rejection-sampling fine-tuning (A3) trains on the model's own Train samples above a reward threshold, at most two per drug; keeping only exact matches would starve it. <!-- src/rlexp/arms.py --> A3 was not run: it needs a starting model with Train hits, and the dense-reward variant is an omission (Section 6). A1 uses the same adapters and learning rate with sequences up to 8,192 tokens (longer rows dropped), batches of 16 rows and two epochs; epoch 2 had the lower Val loss (1.28 at epoch 1, 1.25 at epoch 2), and it took 25 minutes on the A100 against 9 minutes for the three epochs of A2. <!-- data/processed/arms/*.meta.json; Drive logs summary.json of the two SFT runs, copied to runs/sft/ -->

**Online RL (A5).** We wrote a small training loop because the library we considered needed hooks for prompt-level aggregation, batch normalisation of advantages, dropping zero-variance groups and an fp32 tied output head. <!-- docs/plan.md section 6 --> Rollouts are sampled from the model being trained, so no engine mismatch can arise, and the output head runs in 32-bit floats in generation and training. Each step draws distinct Train drugs, one random view each, and samples eight answers per drug (temperature 1.0, up to 128 new tokens; the Val and Test runs allow 256). Rewards are centred within each group, groups with no spread are dropped, and the remaining advantages are divided by their batch standard deviation. Each token is weighted by its advantage over the group's tokens and the number of retained groups, so every drug counts equally. There is no KL penalty, and with one update per rollout batch the importance ratio is 1, so the loss is REINFORCE with a group baseline. Training uses LoRA from the A2 adapter (the untrained model is valid in only 16% of samples), AdamW without weight decay, 5 warm-up steps and gradient clipping at 1.0. The pilot run (R1) uses 24 drugs per step (at least 12 must keep a reward spread), 40 steps, learning rate 2e-5 (one setting; no learning-rate search was run) and a Val evaluation every 10 steps with four samples per prompt; the replicate (R5) uses another seed, up to 100 steps and eight samples per prompt in the evaluations. <!-- runs/rl/R1/summary.json (command); gpu/RL.md -->

**Stops, gates, selection.** A run stops when the collapse score (distinct most-common answers over drugs with a valid answer) stays below 0.7 for three steps, when over 30% of a step's samples hit the cap, or when Val first-valid similarity fails to improve for three evaluations while training reward rises; the collapse rule is switched off for profile-free runs, where one constant answer is the best policy, so R4 and R4b ran their 40 steps. On an A100, sampled-token log-probabilities differed from the training forward pass by a mean of 0.015 to 0.018 per token (mean importance ratio 0.994 to 1.000), the same for a lone prompt and a heavily padded batch, so the gap is 16-bit decoding noise. A three-step smoke test passed, and a resumed run reproduced the schedule and seeds. <!-- docs/plan.md section 6; gpu/RL.md --> The checkpoint entering the confirmatory contrast is the saved step with the highest Val first-valid similarity when re-sampled with vLLM at eight samples and the arm's chosen temperature, ties to the earlier step. <!-- docs/prereg-v1.md section 5 --> The RL results are in Section 4.4.

**Recipe from the literature.** Our RL loop follows choices reported in the scaling study of Khatri et al. (2025, ICLR 2026), which fits sigmoidal compute-performance curves and finds that the loss type and a 32-bit output head raise the asymptote in single-change ablations (inside the full recipe the 32-bit head matters little for a dense 8-billion-parameter model), while aggregation, normalisation and curriculum mostly change speed. We adopt prompt-level aggregation, batch normalisation of advantages, zero-variance filtering and the 32-bit head, but no curve fit: our budget is about two orders of magnitude smaller in RL steps (40 to 100 against 7,400) and three to four in GPU-hours. The group-baseline family differs in details: Dr. GRPO (Liu et al., 2025b) removes the length and standard-deviation normalisation, DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) adds dynamic sampling and overlong reward shaping and drops the KL penalty, and CISPO (MiniMax, 2025) clips importance weights instead of token updates. Schulman and Thinking Machines (2025) report that LoRA matches full fine-tuning in RL when applied to all layers with a learning rate about ten times larger, hence our all-projection LoRA (Hu et al., 2021). Hernandez et al. (2021) fit effective data transferred from pretraining as a power law in fine-tuning data and model size, on loss rather than RL; we treat any extension to our setting as inference and report the size sweep without fitted exponents. <!-- docs/paper/references_audit.md rows 19 to 26 -->

## Appendix E. The controls checklist

A checklist for any text-to-structure or molecule-generation result that is scored by fingerprint similarity. Each item says what to compute, what it costs, why, and what this study measured on its three
evaluation sets: Val (20 drugs in 16 components; the selection data), Test-R (30 drugs in 27 components; random held-out components) and Test-T (41 drugs; a temporal cohort first approved in 2025 and 2026).
Similarity is Morgan count-fingerprint Tanimoto; "delivered similarity" is the similarity of the first valid answer in up to 8 tries. Intervals are wide at these sizes. Sources are files in this repository.
Cost: "CPU" is minutes to a day of CPU work on your own data; "inference" is one more sampling pass; "training" is one more training run.

1. **Tuned constant answer** (CPU). Compute: the one structure (training set, optionally plus a public pool) with the highest mean similarity to the training targets; answer it for every input.
   Why: a score is news only above what an input-free answer earns. Here: reward-optimal constant 0.187 [0.151, 0.229] on Val, 0.199 on Test-T (the best of 1,828 candidate constants there) and 0.159 on
   Test-R (21st of 1,828; the best is 0.171); the Train-only constant 0.176, 0.184 and 0.160. The constant's level moves by 0.04 between splits (results/val_baselines.json, test_t_baselines.json, constant_ceiling*.json).
2. **Random-pair calibration** (CPU). Compute: the similarity of random pairs of reference structures; place each score in that distribution.
   Why: similarity scales are compressed and a score means little without one. Here: mean 0.141, 90th percentile 0.213, 99th 0.274 over 332 structures; 0.187 is the 79th percentile, 0.199 the 85th and the RL
   checkpoint's 0.186 on Test-T the 78th. A scale over the study's drugs, not a null distribution (scripts/random_pair_calibration.py, results/random_pair_calibration.json).
3. **Delivered similarity at a stated cost** (the evaluation pass). Compute: the similarity of the answer a user would get under a fixed policy (first valid of up to 8 tries), with mean tries and generated tokens per answer.
   Why: an arm can raise similarity by paying in tries. Here, Test-T: untrained 4B 0.073 at 5.5 tries and 761 tokens; answer-only SFT 0.131 at 2.0 tries and 92 tokens; RL checkpoint 0.186 at 1.0 tries and
   34 tokens; every constant 0 tokens. Prompt tokens are not counted, which flatters retrieval (results/test_t_baselines.json).
4. **Shuffled-input counterfactual** (inference). Compute: rerun the system with each input replaced by another item's input (same task type and length), score against the original targets, and read an input-deleted
   run through mean similarity, not first-valid similarity. Why: the drop is the part of the score that depends on the input. Here: real minus shuffled delivered similarity for the RL checkpoint +0.012 (Val, p = 0.034),
   +0.004 (Test-T, p = 0.31), +0.013 (Test-R, p = 0.010, which is 0.082 after Holm over the eight Test cells); untrained 9B and 27B +0.040 and +0.049 (Val, p = 0.045 and 0.009). Deleting the input costs the RL checkpoint
   0.018 [0.009, 0.027] of its 0.184 mean similarity on Test-T, 60% of which another drug's input recovers (results/paired_*.json, paired_blank_*.json).
5. **Constant-proof readout** (CPU on the answers you already have). Compute: rank the true target among the split's own N references by similarity to your answer; report the top-1 rate, the mean percentile rank, the share of rows with
   an answer and the number of distinct answers. Why: one fixed answer valid on every row scores exactly 1/N and (N+1)/2N whatever the molecule (failures and ties only lower it; a policy whose answers vary scores them in
   expectation), so a constant cannot pass. Here: chance 0.525 (N=20), 0.512 (N=41), 0.517 (N=30); RL checkpoint 0.473, 0.475, 0.463; nearest-profile copy 0.439, 0.427, 0.396; constants and input-deleted runs sit on chance (results/rank_*.json).
6. **Held out by structure component and by date** (CPU). Compute: join molecules into components (similarity 0.4 or more, or the same ring scaffold), assign whole components to splits, and add a cohort by first approval date.
   Why: near-duplicates across splits reward memorising. Here: 302 components, no cross-split pair reaches 0.4 (largest 0.398), no scaffold is shared, and the date cohort is 41 drugs first approved
   in 2025-26 (data/processed/split_report.json). Approval is not disclosure: 40 of those 41 have a PubChem record 3 to 20 years older (median 10.5).
7. **Validity-only reward control** (training). Compute: train the same recipe with a reward that pays only for a parseable answer. Why: separates learning the output format from learning chemistry.
   Here: 99.5% to 99.8% valid at step 40 but delivered similarity 0.074 to 0.080 on Val, far below the constant 0.187 (results/val_baselines.json, rows rlvalidity-s40-*).
8. **Input-free training control** (training; 0.2 GPU-hours here). Compute: train with the same reward and the input replaced by one fixed string. Why: it shows what the reward gives with no input at all, which is the constant the
   policy can converge to. Here: in two runs the training reward matches the run with the input (0.159 and 0.152 against 0.158 over steps 31 to 40) and Val similarity at step 40 is 0.178 and 0.196 against 0.183 with the input
   (0.195 for the selected checkpoint), by policies that concentrate on few answers (results/paired_val_r4*.json, runs/rl/R4, R4b).
9. **Seeds and selection optimism** (CPU, bookkeeping). State: training seeds, how many candidates were scored on the selection split, and the spread when the same checkpoint is sampled again.
   Why: the best of many candidates on 20 drugs is biased upward. Here: the reported RL checkpoint is the best of 21 Val candidates (0.149 to 0.195, median 0.183) and delivers 0.186 on Test-T and 0.168 on
   Test-R; re-sampling it gives 0.1952 and 0.1948 against 0.1949; two RL training runs, one seed each (results/selection.json, results/gate_dryrun.json).
10. **The constant next to every number** (CPU). Report: the constant's score and the ceiling beside each result, and say whether the result is above them. Why: it is the reading that survives review.
    Here: Test-T constant 0.199 = ceiling, RL 0.186 below it; Test-R constant 0.159, ceiling 0.171, RL 0.168; Val constant 0.187, ceiling 0.195, RL 0.195 (selected on it). No system recovers a
    structure: exact match is 0 for every system on every split.
11. **Reward-spread check before training** (inference on the starting policy). Compute: sample eight answers per training prompt from the policy RL will start from, keep the valid ones, and count the prompts whose valid
    answers have a reward standard deviation of at least 0.05; the protocol asked for 30% of prompts. Why: a group-relative method learns only from reward differences within a group, so if valid answers do not differ in reward
    it can learn validity and genericness and little else. Here: at most 15.5% of Val prompts for any run, 5.2% for the SFT start at the RL sampling temperature and 0 to 5.2% for the RL checkpoints; RL ended at a generic
    structure (results/t5.json).
